How Much Does eDiscovery Cost Per Gigabyte in 2026?

Processing runs $25 to $75 a gigabyte and hosting adds $10 to $25 a month — yet the Winter 2026 pricing survey shows the per-gigabyte quote is the smallest part of the bill.

Prime Domain Capital Research  ·  November 19, 2026

Two vendors quoted the same matter in the spring of 2026. One charged $25 a gigabyte to process the data. The other charged $75. Both were established firms, both quotes covered the same collection, and neither vendor was lying. The threefold gap between them is the honest answer to the question in this article's title: eDiscovery costs what the gigabyte is measured against, and the industry has never agreed on the measuring stick.

The best public evidence comes from ComplexDiscovery's Winter 2026 eDiscovery Pricing Survey, published February 23, 2026 with the Electronic Discovery Reference Model. Fifty-three practitioners — law firms, vendors, and corporate legal departments — answered 25 questions about what they charge and pay. On processing at ingestion, the largest group, 39.6 percent, charged $25 to $75 a gigabyte; another 34 percent charged under $25. Nearly three-quarters of the market, in other words, processes a gigabyte for less than $75. That sounds like an answer. It is the beginning of one.

Processing: the cheapest line, and the first trap

The survey asked the same question twice — once at ingestion, once at completion — and the answers moved. At completion, after culling, deduplication, and filtering, the largest group charged under $100 a gigabyte (37.7 percent), but 15.1 percent charged $100 to $150 and 9.4 percent charged more than $150. The gigabyte shrinks as duplicates and junk fall out, and the price per surviving gigabyte climbs. A vendor quoting $25 at ingestion and a vendor quoting $150 at completion can be describing the same job. About one in five respondents — 18.9 percent at ingestion, 22.6 percent at completion — skip per-gigabyte processing prices entirely and use alternative models, which is the market admitting the unit does not fit every matter.

Run the survey's own bands through a 50-gigabyte matter, as the pricing guide Legal Lens did in October 2026: processing at ingestion costs $1,250 to $3,750, once. That is the smallest number on the invoice, and the one buyers negotiate hardest. It is also the number that matters least, because processing is a one-time charge and everything after it recurs.

A decade ago the same line cost more. Processing ran about $30 a gigabyte in 2016, according to an April 2026 pricing review by Platinum IDS, and has fallen roughly 90 percent over ten years as automation and cloud infrastructure did the work. The direction is down. The spread is what survives: a gigabyte of clean email processes cheaply, while a gigabyte of chat exports, voice files, and scanned PDFs does not, and no survey band captures the difference.

Hosting: where the gigabyte becomes a subscription

Hosting is the bill that compounds. Without analytics, 54.7 percent of survey respondents charge under $10 a gigabyte a month, and 30.2 percent charge $10 to $20. With analytics — threading, clustering, concept search — the largest group, 43.4 percent, charges under $15, 32.1 percent charge $15 to $25, and 11.3 percent charge more than $25. On that same 50-gigabyte matter hosted for 18 months, basic hosting stays under $9,000; at $10 to $20 it runs $9,000 to $18,000; with analytics at $15 to $25 it runs $13,500 to $22,500. Processing was a few thousand dollars. Hosting is five times that, and it keeps running until the case ends.

The public price lists confirm the spread. Epiq's UK G-Cloud 14 list, valid from March 5, 2026, prices RelativityOne review hosting at £7.00 a gigabyte a month, early-case-assessment hosting at £2.50, and dormant hosting at £1.50 — and all-in processing at £45.50 a gigabyte for the smallest volumes, falling to £27.65 above one terabyte. Volume is its own discount. Everlaw's federal GSA list from August 2025 prices active data at $25.24 a gigabyte a month on a per-case subscription, with bundle overage rates falling from $17.42 at 500 gigabytes to $7.49 at 50 terabytes, and suspended data at $3.50. The same gigabyte costs three times as much at small volume as at large, before anyone has reviewed a document.

The meter runs on users, too. RelativityOne licensing typically runs $100 to $200 or more per reviewer per month depending on contract size, so a 20-reviewer matter carries $2,000 to $4,000 a month before a single document is coded. DISCO's answer to all of this was structural: collapse the platform into a single per-gigabyte fee that includes its Cecilia AI assistant, deposition management, and analytics. CS Disco reported full-year 2025 revenue of $156.8 million, up 8 percent, with revenue from Cecilia AI and its Auto Review product up more than 600 percent in the fourth quarter of 2025 against the same quarter of 2024 — the market paying, loudly, for the bundled model.

The 2012 anchor: what a gigabyte used to cost

The reference point for all of this is a study from before the cloud era. RAND's 2012 report, Where the Money Goes, by Nicholas M. Pace and Laura Zakaras, drew on 57 large-volume productions from eight Fortune 200 companies. Review consumed about 73 percent of production spending, processing 19 percent, collection 8 percent. The median total came to about $17,507 a gigabyte — roughly $18,000 — with per-gigabyte ranges of $125 to $6,700 for collection, $600 to $6,000 for processing, and $1,800 to $210,000 for review. Average figures cited from the study run $1,332 to collect a gigabyte, $2,793 to process it, and $22,480 to review it. RAND's own arithmetic made the point: review costs would have to fall about 75 percent to match processing, the next-largest component.

Fourteen years later, processing and hosting have fallen while review has not fallen nearly as far — which is why the per-gigabyte question misleads. The gigabyte is cheap to move and store. It is ruinously expensive to read, and reading is where the money still goes. The Winter 2026 survey puts remote managed review most often at $0.50 to $1.00 a document, with 18.9 percent paying above $1.00; generative-AI review most often lands at $0.11 to $0.50. A gigabyte holds roughly 3,500 documents or 10,000 emails by the Information Governance Initiative's rule of thumb, so the review arithmetic on a single gigabyte runs into thousands of dollars no matter which rate you choose.

Where the quote hides the bill

The rest of the invoice is where quotes diverge. Forensic collection runs $250 to $350 an hour for the dominant band — about 57 percent of providers — and contract reviewers earn $25 to $40 an hour or more. Production runs $25 to $75 a gigabyte. Project management bills by the hour. None of it appears in a per-gigabyte processing quote, and all of it appears in the final invoice. The survey's own pricing-model data shows the market has noticed: hybrid and per-document billing tied at 28.3 percent each as the primary structure for AI-assisted review, per-gigabyte took 11.3 percent, and per-token and flat subscription took 5.7 percent each. The gigabyte is losing its status as the unit of account.

Matter type moves the number more than vendor does. An internal investigation with a narrow custodian list and clean email processes at the bottom of every band. A multi-district product-liability matter with chat data, mobile extractions, and foreign-language documents processes at the top — or off the scale entirely, under an alternative model. A regulatory inquiry with a short fuse pays rush rates. The survey's 53 respondents cannot price those differences, and neither can a single number.

The case for ignoring the per-gigabyte rate entirely

There is a serious argument that the per-gigabyte question is the wrong question, and it starts with the unit itself. A gigabyte of email and a gigabyte of video depositions have nothing in common except size; a gigabyte after deduplication can be a tenth of the gigabyte collected; and a gigabyte nobody reviews costs nothing beyond hosting. Vendors know this, which is why the most interesting pricing in the market is not per-gigabyte at all: DISCO's single fee, Everlaw's per-case subscription, flat-rate managed review. The per-gigabyte quote survives because it is easy to compare, not because it is informative — and easy comparisons are how buyers get surprised.

The counter to the counter is that buyers still need a unit, and the gigabyte is the only one every vendor speaks. The fix is not to abandon it but to demand it four ways: ingestion and completion, with and without analytics, at the matter's actual volume, with hosting quoted to the expected life of the case. A quote that gives one of those four numbers is a teaser. A quote that gives all four can be budgeted against.

What to do with the number

Budget the matter in three lines, not one: processing once, hosting monthly to the expected end of the case, review per document. Ask whether processing is priced at ingestion or completion, and get the completion number in writing. Ask what volume tier the hosting rate assumes, and what happens when the matter doubles. Cap the hosting term in the engagement letter, or the case's calendar will decide the bill. And treat the per-gigabyte rate as a check on the vendor, not a property of the data — the same gigabyte has cost $25 and $75 this year, and both prices were real.

The firms that buy eDiscovery well are not the ones that find the cheapest gigabyte. They are the ones that know the gigabyte was never the bill.
