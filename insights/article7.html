TAR vs. Human Review: What Technology-Assisted Review Actually Saves

Predictive coding beat exhaustive manual review on recall and precision in the TREC 2009 experiments, and real matters show 30 to 50 percent savings. The catch is in the validation.

Prime Domain Capital Research  ·  November 19, 2026

On February 24, 2012, Magistrate Judge Andrew J. Peck of the Southern District of New York signed an opinion approving predictive coding in Da Silva Moore v. Publicis Groupe — apparently the first time a federal judge had done so. The case involved about three million documents. Manual review would have cost millions of dollars and taken months. Peck did not call the technology magic. He called it acceptable, and he set the standard that still governs: the method must produce higher recall and higher precision than the alternative, at a cost proportionate to the value of the case. Fourteen years later, the savings vendors promise are larger than ever. The evidence for them is better than ever. And the gap between the two is where the money is lost.

The promise starts with a fact about human reviewers that the industry would rather not print: people disagree with each other, and with themselves. That inconsistency is the foundation every savings claim rests on.

What the experiments showed

The strongest evidence for TAR comes from the Text Retrieval Conference, the NIST-sponsored competition where retrieval systems are scored against human judgments. Maura R. Grossman and Gordon V. Cormack, writing in the Richmond Journal of Law and Technology in 2011, analyzed the TREC 2009 Legal Track and found that technology-assisted processes enabled two participating teams to achieve results superior to exhaustive manual review of the entire collection — superior on recall, on precision, and on F1, the measure that combines the two. The machine did not merely cost less. On that dataset, it found more of the responsive documents and made fewer false calls than the humans who reviewed everything.

The reason is visible in the study that haunts every review manager. Herbert L. Roitblat, Anne Kershaw, and Patrick Oot examined a real second-request review — the Justice Department's demand for documents on Verizon's $8.44 billion acquisition of MCI — and had two fresh teams re-review what the original contract attorneys had coded. The re-review teams agreed with the original review on only about 76 percent and 72 percent of documents, and with each other on about 70 percent. When only relevance determinations were counted, the overlap between the two manual productions was roughly 16 percent. The two automated systems tested in the same study overlapped at 21 and 23 percent. William Webber's analysis of those numbers put a ceiling on the whole exercise: at 28 percent agreement, the maximum measurable precision and recall is about 44 percent. Human review is not a gold standard. It is a noisy one, and TAR's first advantage is that the same model gives the same answer to the same document every time.

Grossman and Cormack returned to the problem in 2012 in the Pace Law Review, documenting inconsistent responsiveness assessments across reviewers and arguing that the disagreement is a property of the task, not a failure of effort. The vendors selling TAR rarely lead with this, because it sounds like an attack on lawyers. It is the opposite: it is the reason a validated model, trained on a senior lawyer's decisions and applied uniformly, can outperform a room full of contractors reading at speed.

The cost math, matter by matter

The savings, when they arrive, are large. A Canon Discovery Services white paper modeling typical litigation costs found that a firm using TAR could save 30 percent on total review costs compared with a traditional linear review using contract attorneys. FTI Consulting's published case study from a UK pharmaceutical patent dispute tells a sharper story: continuous active learning cut a review set of more than 500,000 documents to roughly 225,000, and the cost of first-level review fell by approximately 50 percent. The model did not review the documents. It decided which documents deserved a human at all.

The earliest controlled test of that idea came from Winston & Strawn in February 2014. At a large institutional client's request, the firm — Scott M. Cohen, Elizabeth T. Timkovich, and John J. Rosenthal — applied Equivio's predictive-coding technology to about 300,000 documents from a resolved pre-litigation matter that contract attorneys had already reviewed in Relativity. Of the 293,185 documents analyzed, 18 percent had been marked responsive in the human review. The TAR workflow completed in 14 days, exactly as the firm had predicted at the outset. The paper is careful about what it proves: it estimates the time and cost of a defensible TAR against the human review that already happened. It does not claim the human review took a known number of days, because that number was never published. The 14 days is a fact. The comparison is a model.

The most aggressive recent numbers come from the vendors themselves, and should be read as such. Alvarez & Marsal's Forensic Technology Services, writing on Relativity's own blog, ran the same collection of about 30,000 documents twice: first by keyword search and manual review, then with Relativity's aiR for Review. The manual pass took 1,350 hours of reviewer input across 17 working days, with 16.4 percent of coding decisions overturned on quality review. The aiR pass took 9 working days — about 12 hours of actual analysis time, with only 2,200 documents reviewed by hand — and 7.9 percent overturned. A&M reports cost savings of 30 to 70 percent on live cases, and a 99.5 percent elusion rate — the share of relevant documents found — in a Cayman Islands matter. The numbers come from the company that sells the tool, on its own blog.

HaystackID's October 2025 case study makes the same point at larger scale: 891,527 documents, of which 748,612 were eligible for analysis, with richness of just 4 percent. Combining Relativity's aiR with a TAR model cut the set sent for generative-AI analysis from 748,612 to 54,380 — a reduction of more than 90 percent — and only 12,923 documents needed manual review. The study claims 90 percent precision and 95 percent recall. Everlaw's June 2026 case study of an Am Law 200 firm in an insurance coverage dispute reports a similar arc: roughly 600,000 documents narrowed by early case assessment to about 70,000, Coding Suggestions achieving 93 percent precision and recall, the review finishing in under two weeks against six weeks for traditional managed review, at one-fifth of the cost. Three vendors, three case studies, the same shape: the savings are real, and every one of them is self-reported.

TAR 1.0, continuous learning, and the moving target

Part of the confusion is that "TAR" now names three different things. TAR 1.0 — the simple active and passive learning at issue in Da Silva Moore — trains a model on a seed set, then applies it in batches. TAR 2.0, continuous active learning, retrains the model as reviewers code, so the highest-value documents surface first and the model improves while the review is running. The distinction matters because the savings live in the retraining: a model that learns continuously needs fewer human decisions to reach the same recall, and every decision it saves is a contract-attorney hour not billed.

The newest evidence is also the most cautionary. In June 2026, a Redgrave LLP working paper by Robert D. Keeling and colleagues reported that Relativity's aiR for Review reached 87.7 percent recall at 28.6 percent precision on 45,004 documents from the Mallinckrodt collection, against 64.4 percent recall and 39.2 percent precision for Relativity Assisted Learning, the company's machine-learning tool. On October 12, 2026, Cormack, Grossman, and Jeremy Pickens of Elevate posted a reanalysis of the same data (arXiv 2610.16925) arguing the two figures measured different tasks: the contract reviewers had the final say in one arm and not in the other. Scored on a common task, where first-pass coding had the final say for both, RAL reached 64 percent recall and aiR 59.5 percent; unfiltered, RAL reached 97.2 percent against aiR's 88 percent. A continuous active learning model built on the open-source baseline code used at the TREC Total Recall tracks beat both at every point on the gain curve. Their conclusion, in the paper's words: for this task, aiR is "comparable to, but not superior to" the machine-learning workflow. Neither paper is peer-reviewed — Redgrave's is a law-firm working paper, the reanalysis a preprint — which is itself the point. The most-cited comparison in the field this year is two drafts arguing with each other.

The case for the humans

The strongest version of the skeptic's case is not that TAR fails. It is that TAR's savings are quoted gross and spent net. A defensible TAR protocol carries fixed costs that linear review does not: seed-set review by senior lawyers, validation sampling, elusion testing, protocol negotiation with the other side, and an expert to defend the method if it is challenged. On a small collection, those fixed costs can swallow the savings entirely; linear review is cheaper when the population is small enough to read. TAR ranks responsiveness. It does not make privilege calls, and privilege is where a single missed document becomes a waiver fight. Low-richness collections — the HaystackID matter was 4 percent responsive — force the model to spend most of its effort confirming irrelevance. And a model trained by one reviewer inherits that reviewer's blind spots: Roitblat's teams disagreed with each other, so the choice of trainer is itself a decision with consequences.

Then there is the defensibility tax. Judge Peck's own opinion warned that computer-assisted review is "not a magic, Staples-Easy-Button, solution appropriate for all cases," and the Sedona Conference's Principle 6 puts the choice of method on the producing party — which means the producing party owns the validation burden. A firm that cannot produce an elusion rate, a control sample, and a written protocol has not saved money. It has bought a motion to compel. The vendors' case studies rarely price the validation work, because the vendors do not sell it.

What the savings are actually worth

The honest accounting is this: TAR reliably cuts the population humans must read, and the cut is worth 30 to 50 percent of first-level review cost on large matters with the right shape — high volume, moderate richness, a senior lawyer willing to train the model and defend it. Below that threshold, the fixed costs of doing it defensibly eat the savings. Above it, the savings are the reason the method survived fourteen years of skepticism. Generative-AI review has not changed that math; score every new tool against the same recall target you would demand of TAR, on the same task, before you sign a protocol.

Buy the method, not the headline. Negotiate the protocol before collection, not after. Demand the elusion rate, not the accuracy rate — accuracy is easy when 96 percent of the documents are irrelevant. And budget the validation as a line item, because the court will ask for it whether you budgeted for it or not. The technology passed its test in 2012. The procurement of it is still being graded.
