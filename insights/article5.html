The Free Review: Why the Biggest eDiscovery Vendors Just Gave Away Their Best Feature

Relativity put its generative-AI review tools into the standard RelativityOne package at no extra charge. Everlaw followed. DISCO collapsed its pricing into a single per-gigabyte fee. The most expensive line item in litigation just became a loss leader.

Prime Domain Capital  ·  November 2026

The general counsel stared at two quotes for the same review and felt something unfamiliar: neither one charged for the AI. A year earlier, generative-AI document review had been a line item with a price per document and a sales call attached. Now it was included. The vendors had not become generous. They had changed the game, and the first thing to understand about a free feature in enterprise software is who it is free for.

The sequence matters. At Relativity Fest in October 2025, Relativity announced that its aiR for Review and aiR for Privilege generative AI solutions would be included in the standard RelativityOne package at no additional charge starting in early 2026. Everlaw responded by making its single-use EverlawAI Assistant features free. DISCO took a more radical approach, collapsing its entire platform into a single per-gigabyte hosting fee. Three vendors, three versions of the same decision: the review itself is no longer the product. It is the bait.

The announcements, in order

Relativity is the vendor that could afford to move first. It is used by 198 of the Am Law 200 firms and processes data for the majority of Department of Justice investigations, and its aiR for Review reads documents, generates summaries, and produces coding suggestions with source citations. Putting that into the standard package removed the one objection every RelativityOne sales call had been hearing: that the AI cost extra. Everlaw’s answer was narrower but pointed in the same direction — single-use AI assistant features, the ones a reviewer reaches for a dozen times a day, free. DISCO’s answer was structural: stop charging for the pieces and charge for the gigabyte, which is the one thing that only grows.

The pricing surveys caught the transition mid-flight. ComplexDiscovery’s Winter 2026 eDiscovery Pricing Survey, conducted with the Electronic Discovery Reference Model and 53 respondents across law firms, vendors and corporations, found generative-AI review converging on $0.11 to $0.50 a document — against $1.50 to $3.00 for traditional human review. Hybrid models and per-document billing each represent about 28% of primary pricing structures, which is another way of saying there is no consensus model yet. In the Summer 2025 survey, nearly half of respondents — 46% — were not sure what they paid for AI-assisted review. In the Winter 2025 edition, nearly a third reported no familiarity with or use of it at all.

Why free, why now

The vendors are not giving away margin. They are moving it. Review has been the biggest line item in eDiscovery for as long as anyone has measured it — 73% of task spend in RAND’s 2012 study, 64% in 2024, and a projected 52% by 2029 as AI efficiencies bite. ComplexDiscovery projects total eDiscovery spending will grow from $16.89 billion in 2024 to $25.11 billion by 2029 anyway, and other estimates put the market at $18.73 billion in 2025 rising to $20.74 billion in 2026 and $46.06 billion by 2034. The pie is growing. The fight is over who owns the plate it is served on.

Free AI review is a retention weapon. Once a firm’s reviewers, privilege logs and coding history live inside one platform’s AI layer, moving to a rival means rebuilding that history. Data gravity does the selling that discounts used to do. And the meter keeps running elsewhere: per-user licensing for RelativityOne typically runs $100 to $200 or more per reviewer per month depending on contract size, processing runs roughly $15 to $25 per gigabyte, and a large review with twenty or more reviewers can still cost $200,000 to $500,000 or more. The AI is free. The room it works in is not.

The case for paying anyway

There is a serious argument for buying review capability separately, and it starts with defensibility. A free, bundled AI reviewer is a black box whose validation story belongs to the vendor. A best-of-breed tool, bought on its own, comes with its own testing, its own documentation, and its own expert to put on the stand. When the other side challenges a production — and the Winter 2026 survey’s own data shows a market still unsure what it is paying for, let alone what it is getting — the firm that can produce a validation record beats the firm that can produce a marketing page.

The second argument is about the subsidy itself. Free features in enterprise software have a lifecycle: free to win the account, bundled to raise the switching cost, repriced once the switching cost is high enough. The vendors will say the AI is free forever. The history of this market says the meter moves somewhere else — to hosting, to users, to the gigabyte. A buyer who negotiates on the total cost of the matter, not on the price of the AI line, is the one who keeps the savings.

What to do with a free review

Take it, and verify it like you paid for it. Run the AI’s coding against a human sample and keep the error rate. Document the validation the way you would document any other review methodology, because a court will ask for the methodology, not the invoice. And negotiate the rest of the bill — hosting, processing, users — as if the AI were still a line item, because the money did not disappear. It moved upstream, into the parts of the invoice nobody reads.
