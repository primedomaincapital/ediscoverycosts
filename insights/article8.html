How Long Does eDiscovery Take? A Timeline From Preservation to Production

A tight matter can go from preservation to production in two weeks. A large one takes months, and review is where the calendar dies. The stage-by-stage timeline, and the bottleneck that actually sets the date.

Prime Domain Capital Research  ·  November 19, 2026

The opposing counsel wanted production in two weeks. Ten custodians, each generating data across computers, email, cloud storage, Teams, and cell phones — fifty distinct data sources, five platforms. The litigation team stared at the deadline the way teams always do: as if it were a negotiating position. It was not. It was a date, and the work behind it had a shape that no amount of urgency changes. Preservation, collection, processing, review, production. Each stage has a duration, each duration has a cause, and the cause that matters most is the one nobody budgets for — the review, which is where two weeks become two months and two months become a year.

The honest answer to how long eDiscovery takes is that it depends on which clock you mean. A small, well-run matter can move from preservation to production in fourteen days. A large, contested matter can take a year. The difference is not effort. It is volume, and the volume is set by forces the litigation team does not control: how many custodians, how many data sources, how much of what they produce is actually relevant, and whether the other side fights over any of it. The timeline below is the realistic shape of the work, stage by stage, with the numbers that govern each one.

Preservation and collection: days, not weeks

Preservation starts the moment litigation is reasonably anticipated, and it is the one stage with no duration at all — a legal hold is issued, custodians are told to stop deleting, and the clock starts. Collection is the first stage with a real calendar. For a contained matter, forensic collection of ten custodians across computers, Office 365, OneDrive, Teams, and cell phones takes one to three days, with remote collections often beginning the same day the engagement starts. That is the fast case, documented by Computer Forensic Services in a February 2026 matter with exactly that profile: fifty sources, fourteen days end to end, collection finished by day three.

The slow case is slower by an order of magnitude, and the reason is physical. A forensic image of a hard drive copies at roughly 40 gigabytes an hour, and a single laptop drive can take up to eight hours to image. A network share can take days or weeks, because the copy shares resources with the server's ordinary work and cannot be rushed without taking the server offline. A single large EnCase image can take a day to extract even on fast hardware. None of this is anyone's fault. It is the physics of moving data, and it is the first place a deadline dies — not because the team is slow, but because the data is large and the tools are honest about it.

Processing: the cull that decides everything

Processing — extracting email from PST files, indexing documents, running OCR, converting chat and text messages, de-duplicating across custodians — takes two to five days on a contained matter, running in parallel with collection as sources complete. The output of processing is the number that matters most in the whole timeline: how much data survives the cull. Early case assessment typically removes about 70 percent of the collected documents from active review, according to Everlaw's 2022 figures, and that single cut determines whether review takes weeks or months. A matter that collects 250,000 documents — the average in FirstService Residential's portfolio, at 22 gigabytes per matter — reviews 75,000 of them after culling. A matter that skips the cull reviews all 250,000, at roughly one document per minute for a first pass, which is the industry-standard review speed Everlaw still cites. The arithmetic is unforgiving: 75,000 documents at one a minute is 1,250 reviewer-hours. 250,000 is 4,167. The cull is not a nicety. It is the schedule.

Loading the processed data into the review platform takes another day or two, including quality control on document counts, metadata accuracy, and search. On a small matter the platform is ready in a day. On a large one the load itself can take a week, because the data has to be indexed before it can be searched, and indexing is the kind of work that cannot be hurried by adding people. The platform is where the matter becomes visible to the lawyers, and it is also where the first real decision gets made: what to review first, and what to set aside.

Review: where the calendar dies

Review is the stage that eats the timeline, because review is the only stage whose duration scales linearly with volume and whose quality cannot be automated away. A first-pass review runs at about one document per minute — the standard Everlaw cites — which means a 75,000-document review set needs 1,250 reviewer-hours, or about eight reviewers working full-time for a month. Technology-assisted review changes the shape of that curve: predictive coding, validated against a control set, can cut the review population further and focus human attention on the documents that matter. But TAR has its own timeline — building the seed set, training the model, validating the results — and the validation is what the courts actually care about. Judge Peck's 2012 opinion in Da Silva Moore, the first federal approval of predictive coding, set the standard that still governs: the method must produce higher recall and higher precision than the alternative, at a cost proportionate to the value of the case. Meeting that standard takes time, and the time is not optional.

The second reason review eats the calendar is that review is where the case is actually litigated. Privilege review, responsiveness calls, and the arguments over both are not administrative work — they are the substance of the dispute, and they happen document by document. A reviewer who finds a privileged document stops. A reviewer who finds a hot document escalates. A reviewer who finds nothing moves on. The work cannot be parallelized beyond the number of qualified reviewers, and qualified reviewers are the scarcest resource in the process. That is why the fastest matters in the industry are the ones that invested in the cull: FirstService Residential cut its subpoena response from three weeks to under a day by moving collection, review, and production onto a single platform, with a median 15 minutes from upload to processed and ready for review. Compass cut its subpoena response from two weeks to five hours — a 90 percent reduction — the same way. The technology did not make review faster. It made the matter smaller before review started.

Production, and the deadline that actually binds

Production — generating the set in the format the other side requires, TIFF with load files, native format, PDF, or a combination — takes two to three days once review and privilege review are complete. It is the shortest stage and the most unforgiving, because it is the stage the court sees. A production with a privilege slip is a waiver. A production with a formatting error is a do-over. The quality control that precedes it is not optional, and it is the reason production takes days rather than hours even when the data is ready.

The deadline that actually binds is rarely the one the parties set for themselves. In the Northern District of Illinois and the District of Arizona, the Mandatory Initial Discovery pilot requires production within 100 days of filing — initial discovery responses 30 days after a responsive pleading, production 40 days after that, no exceptions, no extensions. That is the fast end of the spectrum, and it exists precisely because the ordinary timeline is so much longer. In a typical federal case, discovery runs six to twelve months from the Rule 26(f) conference, and document review alone can take several months or more. The 100-day pilot is not the norm. It is the proof that the norm is slow.

The counterargument is that speed is not the goal, and the teams that treat it as one lose cases. A review done in two weeks because the cull was aggressive is a review that missed documents, and a missed document is a sanction, a waiver, or a lost motion. The fastest defensible timeline is not the fastest timeline. It is the fastest timeline that survives a challenge, and the challenge comes from the other side, the court, or both. The CFSI fourteen-day matter worked because the collection was complete, the processing was thorough, and the review was focused — not because anyone skipped a step. Speed in eDiscovery is a function of preparation, not of hurry, and the preparation starts before the complaint is filed.

A general counsel asking how long eDiscovery will take should ask a different question first: how much data is there, and how much of it is actually relevant? The answer to the first question sets the collection and processing calendar. The answer to the second sets the review calendar, which is the calendar that matters. Budget two weeks for a contained matter with a good cull, three to six months for a large one, and a year if the other side fights over privilege or scope. And budget the cull first — because the cull is the only stage that makes every later stage shorter, and it is the only stage where spending a week saves a month.
